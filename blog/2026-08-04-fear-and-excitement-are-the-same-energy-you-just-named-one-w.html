Fear and Excitement Are the Same Energy — You Just Named One Wrong

Your body doesn't know the difference between fear and excitement. It's the same rush, the same signal. The label you choose determines everything.

← Back to the dispatch

When I was twelve years old, I saw Tony Robbins on TV. I didn't know who he was. But something inside me said, present tense, no hesitation: that's who I am. Not who I want to be. Not who I might become if I work hard enough. Who I am.

The feeling in my body when that knowing arrived was electric. Chest tight. Palms buzzing. Heart rate elevated. If you had asked me to describe it, I could have easily called it fear. But I didn't. I called it recognition.

Years later, standing in the wreckage of my company after the collapse, I felt the exact same sensation. Same tightness. Same buzz. Same elevated heart rate. This time I called it fear. And I acted accordingly. I contracted. I protected. I moved from scarcity instead of certainty.

Here's what I didn't understand then: fear and excitement are physiologically identical. Your nervous system doesn't differentiate. It sends the same signal. Elevated cortisol. Increased adrenaline. Heightened awareness. The energy is neutral. The label is not.

We've been conditioned to treat fear as the responsible choice and excitement as naive optimism. Fear gets called realism. Excitement gets called reckless. So when the body sends that signal, we default to the story we've been told is safer. We name it fear, and then we wonder why we keep attracting what we're afraid of.

The Label Determines the Direction

Both fear and excitement are signals of importance. Both say: this matters. But fear moves you toward what you're trying to escape. Excitement moves you toward what you already know is yours. Same energy. Different vector. The one you name is the one you empower.

I've made sales calls from both states. The energy in my body felt almost identical. But the call made from fear sounds like convincing. The call made from excitement sounds like certainty. The client can't explain the difference, but they feel it. And they respond accordingly.

This isn't about pretending fear doesn't exist. It's about recognizing that the sensation you've been interpreting as a warning might actually be an invitation. The tightness in your chest before you hit send on that email. The buzz before you make the offer. The elevated heart rate before the conversation that could change everything. What if that's not your body telling you to stop? What if it's your soul saying pay attention — this is the edge of something real?

I write about this more in the Win The Day dispatch, but the shift isn't in the feeling. It's in the naming. You don't have to eliminate fear. You just have to stop letting it decide what the signal means.

The next time your body sends that electric charge, pause before you label it. Ask yourself: is this fear trying to protect me from danger, or is this excitement that I've been taught to call something else? The energy is the same. The outcome depends entirely on what you call it.
