When Your Business Problem Becomes an Existential One

The real fear wasn't losing the company. It was the terror of succeeding at everything and still feeling hollow.

← Back to the dispatch

I wasn't building a company. I was building evidence of my own worth.

That's the sentence that arrived on the bathroom floor at two in the morning. Not dramatic. Just a man finally unable to outrun the question he'd been avoiding for years.

When the business was flying—multi-million dollar profits, sixty-five staff, deals closing across two states—I felt like I was someone. When the partner left and the losses mounted and the staff shrank to eighteen and my personal savings evaporated, I didn't just feel like I was losing a business. I felt like I was disappearing.

That's the identity trap. When your worth becomes inseparable from your output, a business problem stops being a business problem. It becomes an existential one.

The deals weren't just deals. They were proof I mattered. The growth wasn't just growth. It was evidence I deserved to take up space. Every closed transaction was another brick in the wall I'd built between me and the terror that maybe, underneath it all, I was nobody.

So when the business started collapsing, it wasn't just the numbers that scared me. It was the question underneath: if I'm not this, then what am I?

The Real Fear Underneath

The real fear wasn't failure. It was invisibility. The terror of succeeding at everything and still feeling hollow. Of climbing the mountain and discovering there's nothing at the top but more mountain.

I'd spent years solving for the wrong variable. I thought the problem was revenue, or team size, or market conditions. But the real problem was that I'd outsourced my identity to outcomes I couldn't control. I'd made my worth conditional on things that could be taken away.

When you do that, every business setback becomes a referendum on your right to exist. Every lost deal is personal. Every rough quarter is evidence you were fooling yourself all along. You can't make clean decisions from that place. You're not solving problems. You're trying to avoid annihilation.

The bathroom floor was the moment I finally saw the operating system running underneath everything. I wasn't trying to build something. I was trying to prove something. And the thing I was trying to prove could never be proven that way.

Because worth isn't earned. It just is. The company can grow or shrink. The account can be full or empty. The deals can close or fall through. And none of it changes what you are.

This is what I write about in the Win The Day dispatch every week—the hidden operating systems that run underneath our businesses and our lives. The patterns we can't see until pressure forces us to look.

The business problem you're facing might not be a business problem at all. It might be the question you've been too afraid to ask: who am I when the wins stop coming?

The answer doesn't live in the next closed deal. It lives in the space before you need one.
